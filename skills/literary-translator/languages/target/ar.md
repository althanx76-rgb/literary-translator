# Arabic as the target language (`ar`)

> Status: first draft. Native review welcome (see CONTRIBUTING.md).

Read this file when translating **into** Arabic, together with the source-language file. Checker option: `--to ar`.

## Register and variant

Modern Standard Arabic (fuṣḥā) for the narration. For dialogue the default is Standard Arabic in a more spoken register; a dialect (Egyptian, Levantine…) only if the author and the publisher choose a specific market. Record the choice in the brief.

## Grammar that forces a choice

Arabic marks gender on verbs, adjectives and the second person (أنتَ / أنتِ) and has a dual: information that the source language leaves implicit (a "you" said to two people) must be decided. If the author keeps a gender hidden, rephrase and flag it in the notes.

## Dialogue and punctuation

- « » or “ ”, one system for the book; dialogue often opens with a dash.
- Arabic punctuation ، ؛ ؟
- Digits: Western (0-9) or Eastern Arabic (٠-٩) according to the market, never mixed.
- Right-to-left text: in .docx files, paragraphs with right-to-left direction.
- The script flags Latin punctuation (, ; ?) in Arabic text and mixed digit systems.

## Religious formulas

إن شاء الله, الحمد لله and والله are common fillers, but in the mouth of a Soviet or an Italian character they add a color the original doesn't have: use them only where the original has an equivalent ("God willing", "thank God").

## Swearing

Standard Arabic has few vulgarities with the force of the strongest swearing in European languages; the strongest are dialectal, and blasphemy is a very heavy taboo. The directive on crude language applies: look for the same force and, where Standard Arabic is not enough, propose a solution to the author (a strong standard insult, or the line in dialect) and record it.

## Names

Foreign names in Arabic script follow established spellings (كييف for Kyiv) and are recorded in the glossary. In the glossary, terms are also found when attached to prefixes and prepositions.

## Italics and emphasis

Italics are not part of Arabic typographic tradition: emphasis uses bold or quotation marks. The script doesn't compare italics.
