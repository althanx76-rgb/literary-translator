# English as the target language (`en`)

> Status: first draft. Native review welcome (see CONTRIBUTING.md).

Read this file when translating **into** English, together with the source-language file. Checker option: `--to en-us`, `--to en-gb`, or `--to en` when the variant is not yet decided.

## Variant

One variant for the whole book: spelling (color/colour, realize/realise), vocabulary (apartment/flat), idioms (knock on wood/touch wood), punctuation. Default: US. The script flags British forms in a US text, US forms in a UK text, and mixtures of the two. Oxford spelling (British spelling with -ize) is acceptable in a UK text only if the publisher uses it.

## Dialogue and punctuation

| Variant | Dialogue | Other |
|---|---|---|
| US | “…”, with the line's punctuation inside the quotation marks | a quotation inside a line: ‘…’; new speaker, new paragraph |
| UK | ‘…’ (more common in novels) or “…”, one choice for the book | the line's punctuation inside the quotation marks |

- Curly apostrophes (’) throughout, unless the brief says otherwise.
- Dialogue introduced by dashes (as in Spanish, Portuguese, Russian, and some Italian and French editions) becomes dialogue in quotation marks.
- The em dash marks an interrupted line ("I didn't—"); an ellipsis marks a line that trails off. Don't use the em dash for asides where the original has commas, parentheses or a colon: the script compares the number of em dashes with the original.

## Dialogue tags

"Said" and "asked" are almost invisible in English, while a run of varied verbs ("he retorted", "she opined", "he interjected") reads as amateurish. When the source verb serves only variety, use "said" or "asked", or drop the tag if the speaker is clear; keep the specific verb when it carries information (whispered, shouted, stammered, hissed).

## Forms of address

English has no familiar/polite distinction. Compensate with first names and surnames, titles (sir, ma'am, Doctor, Comrade), register and contractions. The switch to the familiar form becomes an equivalent gesture ("Call me Anna.") or the move from surname to first name.

## Swearing

Religious oaths ("Jesus Christ", "God damn it") weigh much less in English than blasphemy does in Italian or Spanish: the same blow usually needs a sexual or scatological word. Keep the force of the original, never more.

## Idioms with a strong local color

- Sports idioms ("out of left field", "ballpark figure", "step up to the plate", "it's not cricket", "a Hail Mary") jar in stories set outside the English-speaking world.
- Some idioms are dated: "mum's the word" and "keep it under your hat" sound old-fashioned in the mouth of a young character.
- "Break a leg" belongs to the stage: said before a danger, it turns into a macabre joke.

## Stock phrases to avoid

Translators fill gaps with the stock phrases of English genre fiction. Unless the original has exactly that image, don't write: "a breath he didn't know he was holding", "the weight of [something] settled", "something shifted", "the silence stretched", "barely above a whisper", "for what felt like an eternity", "a wave of [emotion] washed over", "couldn't help but", "the air was thick with", "palpable", "a testament to", "tapestry", "the ghost of a smile", "eyes that held". The script flags them.

## Names and transliteration

Transliterate from non-Latin alphabets with English conventions (Russian Щербина → Shcherbina), not with those of the source language.

## Chapter labels

Chapter 3, Prologue, Epilogue.
