# Italian as the source language (`it`)

> Status: first draft from the original Italian edition of this skill. Native review welcome (see CONTRIBUTING.md).

Read this file when translating **from** Italian, together with the target-language file. The renderings in the examples are into English; what carries over to any target language is the function of each word.

## Discourse markers and interjections

«Dai», «boh», «mah», «mica», «magari», «figurati», «anzi», «insomma», «ecco», «allora», «pure», «va be'» and «macché» carry the tone of the line and change function with the context. They have no fixed rendering:

- «dai»: "come on" (urging), "oh, come on" (protest), "go on" (encouragement);
- «boh»: "no idea", "dunno", "who knows", often with a gesture if the character is laconic;
- «magari»: "maybe" (possibility), "I wish" / "if only" (wish); when the original plays on both senses, look for a form that keeps both ("with any luck" can be hope or bitter irony);
- «figurati»: "don't mention it" (answering thanks), "not at all" (reassurance), "as if" (sarcasm);
- «mica»: strengthens a negation; «non è mica scemo» → "he's no fool";
- «insomma»: "in short", "anyway"; as an answer («Come stai?» «Insomma.») → "Could be better.";
- «va be'»: "fine", "whatever", "oh well".

If they all fall away, the dialogue goes stiff and the characters lose their voice.

## Dialogue verbs

Italian varies the verbs that introduce dialogue a great deal (rispose, replicò, ribatté, soggiunse, sibilò). When the verb serves only variety, follow the target language's norms for dialogue tags (target file); keep it when it carries information.

## Forms of address

- **tu** (familiar), **Lei** (polite: third person singular, often capitalized), **voi** (plural; in some regions and periods also a polite singular). The switch from Lei to tu is a narrative event: record in the brief who uses which form with whom, and where it changes.
- Don't confuse «Lei» of courtesy with «lei», the feminine pronoun ("she").
- Capitalized courtesy forms (Lei, La, Le, Suo…) are not names: the script ignores them when it lists name candidates.

## Grammar that reveals or hides

- **Gender.** Adjectives and past participles carry gender: «Sono stanca», «sei arrivato». One adjective can reveal the gender of a narrator or of an unnamed speaker. Into English the information disappears: if the scene needs it, recover it elsewhere; if the author is hiding it, don't add it in a gendered target language.
- **Dropped subject.** Italian usually omits the subject pronoun. Make clear who acts only as far as the target language requires, without revealing what the author leaves in the shadow.
- **Tenses.** The passato remoto and the imperfetto don't map one to one onto the English simple past and past continuous; the habitual imperfetto often becomes "would" or "used to". The narrative tense of the original stays.
- **Altered nouns (alterati).** «Casetta», «ragazzaccio», «omone», «vecchietto» ask for the precise word ("cottage", "rascal", "big man", "little old man"), not always "little" or "big".
- **Elegant variation.** Italian prose avoids repeating a noun («il vecchio… l'anziano… l'uomo»): check that in translation the synonyms don't look like different people.
- **Suspension points.** Italian often uses «…» for a line that is cut off; English uses an em dash for an interruption and an ellipsis for trailing off. Decide which one it is.

## Swearing

Italian blasphemies (bestemmie) are extremely strong, and English has no religious oath of comparable force ("Jesus Christ" or "God damn it" weigh much less): the same blow often needs a word from another area. In Spanish, religious swearing has comparable force. «Vaffanculo» is "fuck off" or "fuck you", depending on the line, not "go to hell".

## Written speech

Text-message spellings («nn», «cmq», «xké», «tvb») are rendered with the written-speech habits of the target language, not letter by letter.

## False friends, grouped by target language

They work in both directions: when translating into Italian, read them the other way round ("eventually" is «alla fine», not «eventualmente»).

**English:** eventualmente ≠ eventually · attualmente ≠ actually · educato ≠ educated · sensibile ≠ sensible · morbido ≠ morbid · fastidioso ≠ fastidious · libreria ≠ library · parenti ≠ parents · argomento ≠ argument · pretendere ≠ pretend · simpatico ≠ sympathetic · rumore ≠ rumour · fattoria ≠ factory · magazzino ≠ magazine · confrontare ≠ confront · ignorare (not to know) ≠ ignore · terrificante ≠ terrific · disgrazia ≠ disgrace · delusione ≠ delusion · lunatico ≠ lunatic · suggestivo ≠ suggestive · consistente ≠ consistent · annoiato ≠ annoyed · geniale ≠ genial · candido ≠ candid · assistere a ≠ assist · patente ≠ patent · preservativo ≠ preservative · attendere ≠ attend · novella ≠ novel · romanzo ≠ romance · bravo ≠ brave · realizzare ≠ realize (to become aware) · patetico ≠ pathetic (in English often "pitiful").

**Spanish** (words that look Italian): burro (donkey), salir (to go out), largo (long), caldo (broth), embarazada (pregnant), guardar (to keep), subir (to go up), topo (mole), esposas (also handcuffs).

**French:** entendre (to hear), salir (to dirty), fermer (to close), cave (cellar), rumeur (rumour), machine (machine; a car is a voiture).

**German:** kalt (cold), brutto (gross, as in gross weight).

**Portuguese:** esquisito (strange), cena (scene), esperto (clever, cunning).

## Banned words

If the author bans some Italian words (for example «cruciale», «testimonianza», «intricato», «vibrante»), don't reintroduce them through their target-language equivalents where the original doesn't have them.
